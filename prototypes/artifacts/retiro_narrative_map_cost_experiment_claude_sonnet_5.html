Your Retiro Walk — A Nature Quest

Ah, and here we are — Retiro Park, Madrid, a green cathedral in the heart of the city, where the ordinary business of nature carries on in full view of the joggers, the lovers, the ice-cream vendors. Let us step quietly into it.

Our journey begins, fittingly, with a flash of black and white. There — in the branches above — the black-billed magpie. Watch closely, and in the right light, that seemingly simple plumage ignites: iridescent blues and greens ripple across wing and tail, like oil on water. This is no ordinary corvid. Magpies are among the most intelligent birds on the planet, and here, in pairs and small chattering gangs, they seem to know it, surveying their kingdom with an almost proprietorial air.

We move on, following a fainter clue — not a sight, but a sound. A sibilant hiss drifts from somewhere in the canopy. This is the calling card of the Iberian green woodpecker, a bird found nowhere else on Earth but this peninsula. Green and grey, with just a whisper of black on the head and a curious dusky ring circling each eye, it is a master of concealment. Often, the voice is all we get. And that is enough.

Now the path opens toward water, and the character of our walk changes entirely. Gliding across the lake's surface, bold as brass, comes the Egyptian goose — a traveller with an extraordinary pedigree. Its ancestors graced the tomb walls and temple friezes of ancient Egypt; its descendants, escaped and thriving, now call this Madrid lake home.

Further along the shore, an altogether more dramatic sight awaits. Black plumage, a bill of brilliant red — the black swan, a wanderer from Australia's far southern waters, nomadic by nature, led here by the whims of climate and circumstance thousands of miles from home. Yet watch the pair drifting together, and you sense an ancient, universal bond: swans mate for life, sharing every duty of raising their young.

And so our walk closes as so many good walks do — among mallards. Unassuming, endlessly adaptable, gathered here in sociable rafts upon the water, they are perhaps the most successful waterfowl on the planet. A humble, fitting finale to a small safari in the middle of a great city.
